Sewage Backup in Your Home: Health Risks and Cleanup Steps

Sewage water carries bacteria, viruses, and parasites that can make you seriously ill. Don't touch it, don't try to clean it yourself — keep everyone away and call professionals who handle black water safely.

Of all the water damage calls, sewage backups are the ones where I most want homeowners to do nothing. Not because the situation isn't urgent — it is — but because the instinct to grab a mop and start cleaning puts your health at risk. Sewage is classified as Category 3 "black water," the most hazardous type, and cleaning it properly requires protective equipment, containment procedures, and disinfection that no homeowner setup can match.

What Makes Sewage Backup So Dangerous?

Sewage isn't just dirty water. It can contain bacteria like E. coli and salmonella, viruses including hepatitis A and norovirus, parasites, and fungi. Exposure happens through skin contact, inhaling aerosolized droplets, or accidentally ingesting contaminated water — which is easier than you'd think when you're mopping and splashing. People with weakened immune systems, young children, elderly family members, and pets are at the highest risk.

The danger isn't only the visible water, either. Sewage soaks into porous materials — carpet, padding, drywall, insulation, upholstered furniture — where pathogens can persist. A surface that "looks clean" after a DIY wipe-down can still be contaminated underneath. That's why professionals remove affected porous materials rather than trying to clean them.

How Do You Know It's Sewage?

Sometimes it's obvious: water backing up through toilets, tubs, or floor drains with a foul odor and dark color. But gray areas exist. Water that came up through a floor drain during heavy rain may be a mix of stormwater and sewage — treat it as contaminated unless a professional says otherwise. Any water of unknown origin in a basement or lower level should be treated with the same caution. When in doubt, assume the worst and stay out of it.

What to Do Right Now

  1. Keep everyone — including pets — away from the affected area. Close doors to contain it if you can do so without entering the water.
  2. Don't use the plumbing. Running water, flushing toilets, or doing laundry can push more sewage into the house.
  3. Shut off power to the affected area if water is near outlets or electrical equipment — from a dry location only.
  4. Don't run your HVAC system. It can spread airborne contaminants through the ductwork to the rest of the house.
  5. Call for professional help immediately and tell them it's sewage. Sewage backup cleanup crews arrive with the protective gear and equipment this job requires.
  6. Document from a safe distance. Photos for your insurance claim don't require wading in.

What you should not do: mop it, shop-vac it, bleach it and call it done, or throw contaminated items in your regular trash without guidance. Household bleach on a large sewage spill doesn't achieve real disinfection, and it creates a false sense of safety.

What Does Professional Sewage Cleanup Involve?

A proper cleanup follows a strict sequence that protects both the crew and your household:

  • Assessment and containment: The crew identifies the extent of contamination and seals off the area — plastic sheeting, negative air pressure — so contaminants don't spread during the work.
  • Extraction: Standing sewage is pumped out with professional equipment, not mops.
  • Removal of contaminated materials: Carpet, padding, affected drywall, insulation, and other porous materials that absorbed sewage are cut out and disposed of properly. This isn't wasteful — it's the only way to eliminate the contamination.
  • Cleaning and disinfection: All remaining hard surfaces are cleaned and treated with professional-grade disinfectants as part of thorough sanitization and disinfection services.
  • Drying: Commercial dehumidifiers and air movers dry the structure, with moisture readings taken to verify.
  • Verification: The area is checked to confirm it's clean, dry, and safe before containment comes down.

This process exists because shortcuts with black water lead to lingering contamination, persistent odors, and mold growth inside walls. Done right, your home is genuinely safe again — not just visually clean.

Preventing the Next Backup

After cleanup, ask the provider — and a plumber — about the cause. Common culprits in Denver include tree roots invading older sewer lines, grease buildup, collapsed or bellied pipes, and municipal system overload during heavy rain. A sewer line camera inspection can identify the problem. Backwater valves, which let sewage flow out but not back in, are one of the most effective protections for homes with a history of backups. Frequently Asked Questions

Can I clean up a small sewage backup myself?

It's not recommended, even for small amounts. Without proper protective equipment and disinfection procedures, you risk exposure to pathogens and leaving contamination behind. Professional cleanup is the safe choice at any scale.

How long does sewage cleanup take?

It depends on the extent — extraction and material removal often happen the first day, with drying continuing for several days after. Your provider can give you a realistic timeline after assessing the damage.

Will homeowners insurance cover sewage backup?

Many standard policies exclude sewer backup unless you've added a specific endorsement or rider. Check your policy now — before you need it — and ask your agent about adding coverage if you don't have it.
