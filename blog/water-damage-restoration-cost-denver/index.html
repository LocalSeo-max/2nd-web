How Much Does Water Damage Restoration Cost in Denver?

There's no single price for water damage restoration — the cost depends on how much water, what kind of water, what it touched, and how long it sat. Here's an honest breakdown of the factors so you can understand any estimate you're given.

Everyone wants a number. But any website that quotes you a flat price for "water damage restoration" is guessing — a burst supply line in a powder room and a flooded finished basement are entirely different jobs. What I can give you, honestly, is how professionals build an estimate. Once you understand the pieces, you'll be able to look at any quote and know whether it makes sense.

Factor 1: The Size of the Affected Area

This is the most obvious driver. One wet room costs less to handle than water that spread across multiple rooms or floors, simply because there's more water to extract, more square footage to dry, and more material to inspect. Water also travels — what looks like a small kitchen leak may have run under cabinets, into wall cavities, and down into the ceiling below. A proper water damage inspection and assessment uses moisture meters to find everywhere the water actually went, not just where you can see it. The estimate reflects the true footprint, which is sometimes bigger than it first appears.

Factor 2: The Category of Water

Restoration professionals classify water into three categories, and the category changes the job significantly:

  • Clean water (Category 1): From a supply line, a burst pipe, or rainwater that hasn't touched contaminants. This is the simplest to handle — extraction and drying, usually without removing building materials.
  • Gray water (Category 2): From washing machines, dishwashers, or sump pump overflow. It contains some contaminants, so affected porous materials like carpet padding often need to go, and disinfection is required.
  • Black water (Category 3): Sewage, floodwater from outside, or any water that's been sitting long enough to grow bacteria. This is the most involved — contaminated materials must be removed, everything disinfected, and strict safety protocols followed.

As the category rises, so do the labor, protective measures, disposal requirements, and disinfection steps — and the estimate rises with them.

Factor 3: What the Water Touched

Materials matter enormously. Tile over a concrete slab dries very differently than hardwood over a plywood subfloor, and carpet with padding is different again. Drywall wicks water upward and often needs sections cut out; plaster handles it differently. Finished basements cost more to restore than unfinished ones because there's simply more material — flooring, baseboards, drywall, insulation — that may need removal and replacement.

Furniture and belongings are a separate line item. Some can be dried and saved; upholstered items exposed to contaminated water generally can't. A good provider will tell you honestly what's salvageable rather than padding the bill either way.

Factor 4: How Long the Water Sat

Time is the multiplier on every other factor. Water extracted within hours usually means drying and not much else. Water that sat for days — say, from a slow leak behind a wall you didn't notice — means deeper saturation, likely mold growth, and more material removal. This is why fast response matters so much: calling immediately doesn't just limit damage, it directly limits cost. Structural drying services with commercial dehumidifiers and air movers typically run for several days, and the duration depends on how saturated the structure became before drying started.

Factor 5: Drying, Demolition, and Rebuild

A full restoration estimate usually has three phases, and not every job needs all three:

  • Mitigation: Stopping the source, extracting water, and setting drying equipment. Every job needs this.
  • Demolition and remediation: Removing unsalvageable materials and treating for mold if needed. Depends on severity.
  • Rebuild: Replacing drywall, flooring, baseboards, and repainting. This is essentially construction work, and it's often handled as a separate scope.

Ask any provider which phases their estimate covers. A "restoration" quote that only covers mitigation will look cheaper than one that includes the rebuild — compare scopes, not just totals.

How Estimates and Insurance Work Together

Most reputable providers will give you a written estimate after inspecting the damage — be wary of anyone quoting a firm price over the phone without seeing the job. If you're filing a homeowners insurance claim, document everything (photos, videos, a written timeline) and keep receipts for any emergency expenses. Your provider's documentation of moisture readings and damaged materials becomes part of your claim file.

One honest note: insurance typically covers sudden, accidental water damage — a burst pipe, an appliance failure — but not gradual damage from a leak you ignored for months, and not flooding from outside (that requires separate flood insurance). Review your policy and talk to your agent early. Frequently Asked Questions

Why won't companies give me a price over the phone?

Because they'd be guessing. Until someone measures moisture levels and sees what materials are affected, any number is made up — and made-up numbers lead to surprise charges later. A free on-site assessment is the honest starting point.

Will my homeowners insurance cover the restoration?

Often, if the damage was sudden and accidental. Gradual leaks and outside flooding are typically excluded. Every policy differs, so check yours and talk to your agent — and document everything from the start.

What's the cheapest way to keep restoration costs down?

Act fast. Shut off the water, call for help immediately, and don't let water sit. Every hour of delay tends to expand the scope of work — and the bill with it.
