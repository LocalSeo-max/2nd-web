What to Do in the First 60 Minutes After Water Damage

Stop the water, protect yourself, document the damage, and get professional help on the way. In that order. Everything you do in the first hour either limits the damage or makes it worse — here's how to get it right.

After a decade around restoration work, I can tell you the pattern: the homes that recover quickly aren't the ones with the least water — they're the ones where the homeowner acted fast and in the right order. Panic makes people skip steps. This guide is the order that matters.

Step 1: Stop the Water at the Source

Before anything else, stop more water from coming in. If a pipe burst, a supply line failed, or an appliance is leaking, find your main water shutoff valve and turn it off. In most Denver homes it's in the basement, crawlspace, garage, or near the water heater — often where the line enters the house.

Do you know where yours is right now, without having to search? If not, go find it today, before you need it. Tag it with a bright ribbon so anyone in the household can find it in a panic. If the water is coming from outside — a storm, snowmelt, or a flooded street — you can't shut it off, so move to the next steps and focus on protecting what's inside.

Step 2: Make Sure It's Safe to Be in There

Water and electricity are a dangerous combination. If water is near outlets, appliances, or your electrical panel, do not walk through it. Go to your breaker box — if you can reach it without standing in water — and shut off power to the affected rooms. If the panel itself is wet or you can't reach it safely, stay out and wait for help.

Second safety rule: if the water smells like sewage, looks murky, or came up through a drain or toilet, treat it as contaminated. That's called black water, and it can carry bacteria and other pathogens. Don't wade through it, don't try to mop it up yourself, and keep kids and pets completely away from it. When you call for help, mention that it's sewage — it changes how the crew handles the job. Step 3: Document Everything Before You Move It

Grab your phone and photograph everything: the source of the water, every wet room, damaged furniture, swollen baseboards, wet carpet, stained ceilings. Take wide shots and close-ups. Then shoot a slow video walkthrough of the whole affected area. This takes five minutes and it's the single most useful thing you can do for your insurance claim later.

Write down what happened and when — the time you discovered it, what you think the source was, and what steps you took. Memory gets fuzzy fast, especially under stress. If you end up filing a claim, adjusters want a clear timeline, and your notes will be more reliable than your memory a week from now.

Step 4: Move Valuables — Carefully

Once you've documented, move what you can to dry ground: rugs, electronics, important papers, photos, and small furniture. Put furniture legs on foil or wood blocks if the floor is wet — this prevents staining and makes the eventual water extraction work faster. Don't move anything heavy enough to injure yourself, and don't touch anything in standing water if there's any chance of electrical hazard.

If you have fans, you can point them at the wet area to start air movement — it helps, though it won't replace professional drying equipment. Don't use your household vacuum on water, and don't rip up carpet or tear out drywall yourself. Well-meaning demolition usually destroys evidence your insurer needs and can spread contamination.

Step 5: Call for Professional Help Immediately

Here's the part people delay, and it's the costliest delay of all. Water doesn't sit still — it wicks up drywall, seeps under flooring, and soaks into subfloors and framing. Within the first day, damp materials can start growing mold. The difference between calling now and calling tomorrow is often the difference between drying and demolition.

When you call, have this ready: where the water is, roughly how much, what the source was (or that you don't know), whether it's clean water or sewage, and whether the power is on or off in that area. That information helps the provider arrive with the right equipment. What Not to Do in the First Hour

  • Don't wait to "see if it dries on its own." It won't — not inside walls and under floors.
  • Don't use electrical appliances in or near standing water.
  • Don't attempt sewage cleanup yourself. Contaminated water needs professional handling.
  • Don't throw away damaged items before documenting them. Your insurer may want to see them.
  • Don't turn your HVAC up to "dry things out" if there's any chance of contamination — it can spread contaminants through the ductwork.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I call my insurance company or a restoration company first?

Call the restoration company first to stop the damage from spreading, then call your insurer. Mitigation can't wait for claims paperwork, and insurers actually expect you to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage.

How fast does mold start growing after water damage?

Mold can begin growing on damp organic materials within a day or two, which is why fast extraction and drying matter so much. The sooner professionals start the drying process, the less chance mold gets a foothold.

Can I stay in my home during water damage cleanup?

Often yes, for clean-water incidents limited to one area. For sewage backups, major flooding, or situations where power must stay off, you may need to relocate temporarily. The provider can advise based on what they find.
